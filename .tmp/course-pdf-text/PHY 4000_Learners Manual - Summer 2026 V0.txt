A word about Cosmology and our course title 
Over the course of the semester, we will examine many facets of Cosmology, which is the general backdrop 
word covering our course Black holes, Big Bangs and the Cosmos. The purist might note that there is only 
one Big Bang, however recent theoretical work has discussed the possibilities of the “Big Bang” happening 
endlessly in the so-called Multiverse. 
To begin, however, it may be most useful to review the usual definition of the term Cosmology:  
A study of the origin, evolution, and eventual fate of the universe (Wikipedia).

Course Policies 
Learners are responsible for making themselves familiar with the College’s policies and rules outlined 
in the Learner Handbook; these include the Algonquin College Code of Conduct which requires, in

misinterpreted. Be considerate and clear!

The Learner Handbook is an essential reference tool which all learners should keep handy at all times.

Discussion Participation 
Think of the discussion board as a class discussion: a place to share your opinion, comment on the unit's 
readings, audio and video materials and respond to issues raised by both the professor and your fellow 
classmates. 
Your instructor will lead off the class discussion with a series of questions to consider. You should 
frame your posts as a response to either the professor’s leading post, or to the comments of a 
classmate (i.e. Use the “reply” button rather than opening a new thread). You must post in a 
minimum of two different  question threads for each unit to receive your credit. You may post as 
many times as you like to develop the class discussion, and the more you participate, the more you 
will get out of the course. 
Your posts must satisfy the following criteria: 
• Clear: Readers must be able to understand the message easily. Avoid chat jargon and

Documentation 
What is Documentation? 
When you incorporate information from other sources into your work, you must  clearly attribute 
that information to its source . Failure to do so can constitute an act of plagiarism. In academic 
writing, we generally incorporate information  from outside sources using one or a combination of 
three techniques: 
1) Quotation 
• The words of the source are reproduced directly in the text and indicated with quotation 
marks. 
 e.g. The immersive reality in Vinge's story is a kind of "electronically created, shared 
hallucination" (Goldstein 2). 
2) Citation without Quotation 
• Details are taken from the source, but the words are not quoted directly. 
 e.g. The emitter would cost 5-10 million dollars US (Goldstein 5), but is not beyond the 
capabilities of today's technology. 
3) Paraphrasing 
• The gist of the source's ideas are presented, but in your own words. 
 e.g. The immersive reality in Vinge's story is electronically created and it operates 

===== PAGE 14 =====
 
like a common hallucination (Goldstein 2). 
• Note: Paraphrasing can be tricky, because it makes it difficult to judge where your own 
ideas end and another's begin. When in doubt, it is always best to quote your source 
directly to remove any ambiguity. 
Finally, these citations in your text will point to a list of references at the end of your assignment, 
called either a 'bibliography,' 'works cited list,' or a 'reference list,' depending on which style you are 
following. 
 e.g. Works Cited 
 
Asimov, Isaac. I, Robot. New York: Ballantine, 1977. 
Goldstein, Harry. "Mike Villa's World." 4 Jul 2004. IEEE Spectrum Magazine. 31 Aug 2009 
<http://www.spectrum.ieee.org/WEBONLY/publicfeature/jul04/0704mvw.ht ml> 
 
Choosing a Citation Style 
For this course, you may use any of the major citation styles. If you are already comfortable  with one, 
use that. If all of this is new to you, choose the one that is used in disciplines that are closest to your 
own: 
• American Psychological Association (APA) style is most commonly used in the social 
sciences. 
• Modern Language Association (MLA) style is used in liberal arts and the humanities. 
• The Chicago Manual of Style is used in history, anthropology, and the information 
sciences. 
You can find information on how to use each of these styles correctly at the sites collected in the folder 
under Documentation in the Blackboard shell. 
 
What is Plagiarism? 
Algonquin College defines plagiarism  as follows: 
Plagiarism, whether done deliberately or accidentally, is defined as presenting someone else's work, in 
whole or in part, as one's own, and includes the verbal or written submission of another work (for 
example, ideas, wording, code, graphics, music, and inventions) without crediting that source. This 
includes all electronic sources (for example, the Internet, television, video, film, and recordings), all  
print and written sources (for example, books, periodicals, lyrics, government publications, 
promotional materials, and academic assignments), and all verbal sources (for example, conversations 
and interviews). 
Plagiarism constitutes an act of academic dishonesty, and carries serious consequences . All learners 
must read the College plagiarism  policy (AA48) and signal their acceptance of the Attestation of 
Academic Integrity before any assignments will be accepted for grading. Any instances of plagiarism, 
or any other form of academic dishonesty will be prosecuted according to the College policies ( be 
warned: your instructor is alert).
